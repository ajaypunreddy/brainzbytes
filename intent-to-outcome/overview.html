CONTEXTWEAVER · OVERVIEW

Intent to Outcome

What we are showing: a person asks for something in plain words, and the platform turns it into a safe, finished result. Safety checks can be added, removed or tightened while it runs, with no redeploy, and the result stays the same.

In plain English: Think of AI agents and tools as kids. They are helpful and fast, but you don't hand them the car keys. ContextWeaver is the house rules: who they may talk to (ID), what books they may read (RAG), what they must never do (Guardrails), and when a grown-up must say yes (Governance). Parents can change the rules at any time without rebuilding the house.

1. The example: Maria refunds order #4821

Maria is a support agent at Contoso. She signs in and types: "Refund order #4821, the item arrived broken." The refund is $250.

  1. Understand the ask. The platform reads Maria's request and works out that it needs a refund.
  2. Plan it. It picks the refund workflow, the agents and the tools (MCPs) it needs: orders, payments and email.
  3. Check every step. The four checks below run before and after each step.
  4. Do it. The refund is prepared. Maria confirms, her manager approves, and then it is sent.
  5. Prove it. An evidence pack records who, what, which rules and which version.

2. The four checks (the "verticals")

3. Change the checks, keep the journey

Each check is a switch, not code. Admins turn a rule on or off, or tighten it, in the console. The next request picks it up within seconds. Nothing is rebuilt or redeployed.

  • Add a rule: a new rule says "block refunds to a new address". It applies from the next request.
  • Remove a rule: a noisy check is switched off. Maria's refund still completes the same way.
  • Something goes wrong: one bad tool can be switched off instantly. The workflow uses a backup or pauses for a human, so the problem doesn't spread.

Why the outcome stays the same: Maria's intent (refund #4821) and the workflow do not change. Only the checks around it change, so the result is the same, just safer or quicker.

5. How the three levels become one (priority)

Before every request, the platform merges the three levels into one rule set. When rules disagree, five tie-breakers decide, in this order:

  1. Locked rules always win. If the Org locks a rule, nobody below can turn it off.
  2. No beats yes. If any level says deny, the answer is deny.
  3. Strictest limit wins. Org $1,000 and Group $500 means the limit is $500.
  4. Lower levels can tighten, never loosen. A group or user can add an approval but can't remove one.
  5. Knowledge is merged, closest first. All sources are kept. User notes come first, then team documents, then company policy.
Result: Maria's compiled rules for order #4821 ($250)
  • ID Signed in + MFA + Support Agent role (all pass)
  • RAG Her notes → Returns FAQ → Refund policy
  • GR Limit $500 · cards masked · confirm before sending
  • GOV $250 is over $100, so manager approval is required
Outcome: the refund is prepared, Maria confirms, her manager approves, and then it is sent. The evidence pack is stamped with the rule-book version.

6. Why it matters

  • Safe by default: every step is checked, and nothing runs anonymously.
  • Fast to fix: turn off a bad tool or rule in seconds, with no redeploy.
  • Fair and clear: company rules always hold, and teams and people can only make them stricter.
  • Provable: every outcome comes with evidence of who, what and which rules applied.
